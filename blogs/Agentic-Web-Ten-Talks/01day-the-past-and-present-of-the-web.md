# Agentic Web — Ten Talks · Day 1: From the World Wide Web to Super-Platforms — The Historical Inevitability of Web Enclosure

> **Core question**: Why was a network born to enable “connection” bound to become platformized and closed?

In 1989, a British scientist named Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, submitted a seemingly unremarkable proposal titled “Information Management: A Proposal.” His supervisor wrote “vague but exciting” on the proposal.

That “vague” proposal ultimately changed the world. It was the beginning of what we now call the World Wide Web.

More than thirty years later, when we look back at this history, we find a puzzling paradox: **Why did an open network born to enable connection ultimately become dominated by platforms and data silos?**

This is not a story about moral choices. It is a story about the evolution of productive forces. Understanding this history is a prerequisite for understanding why the AI era needs a new Internet.

## The Main Ideas in This Article

- **Why it is called the Web**: Tim Berners-Lee's original vision was that “connection is power”—that people should be free to connect information and connect with one another.
- **Why HTTP succeeded**: simplicity, openness, permissionlessness, and reliance on existing infrastructure—four principles that made the Internet revolution possible.
- **The Web 2.0 turning point**: the move from openness to enclosure was not a betrayal, but an inevitable result of optimizing for human attention and operational capacity.
- **Why the Semantic Web failed**: the high cost of manual annotation, the lack of commercial incentives, and an overly complex technology stack—three mountains that crushed a beautiful vision.
- **The limits of Web3**: it reduced the “trust problem” to an “on-chain problem,” ignoring the realities of user experience and productivity.
- **A key judgment**: enclosure was not a conspiracy, but the optimal solution under the conditions of the old productive forces—economies of scale drove the formation of platform monopolies.

## I. The Web's Original Vision: Connection Is Power

When many people first hear the name “World Wide Web,” they wonder: why a “web”?

Tim Berners-Lee's answer was simple: **because information should not be arranged as a tree; it should be arranged as a web.**

Before the Web, most information systems were hierarchical—like a library classification system or a file-system directory tree. But information in the real world has never been organized that way. A paper may span several disciplines; a person may play several roles; relationships between information look more like an intricate web.

**Hyperlinks** were Tim Berners-Lee's answer. Through URLs and HTML, anyone could create a web page and link it to any other page on the Internet. Information was no longer trapped in isolated islands; it could flow freely through links.

The idea behind this was **“connection is power.”**

### The Original Meaning of “Connection Is Power”

When the Web was born, “connection is power” had three meanings:

1. **Whoever can be connected can be seen**: once your page was on the Internet, anyone in the world could access it.
2. **Whoever can create connections can organize knowledge**: through hyperlinks, anyone could build their own network of knowledge.
3. **Whoever can connect freely has autonomy**: no intermediary and no permission were required.

This idea may sound old-fashioned today, but more than thirty years ago it was revolutionary.

More importantly, the early Web was **infrastructure, not a platform**. It belonged to no company and was controlled by no institution. Anyone could use it freely.

**Openness was the starting point, not a slogan.**

## II. Web 1.0: Why Did HTTP Succeed?

If hyperlinks are the soul of the Web, then the HTTP protocol is its skeleton.

Why did HTTP succeed? Why does it remain a foundation of the Internet more than thirty years later?

The answer can be summarized in four key conditions:

### 1. Simplicity

HTTP's core idea is extremely simple: the client sends a request, and the server returns a response. That's it.

The earliest HTTP/0.9 specification was only one page long. A request was one line: `GET /index.html`; the response was simply the HTML document. There were no request headers, status codes, or complex handshakes.

This extreme simplicity meant that anyone could quickly understand and implement HTTP. You did not need a PhD, expensive equipment, or even specialized training—if you knew a little programming, you could build a Web server.

**Simplicity lowered the barrier; a low barrier brought in more participants; more participants built the ecosystem.**

### 2. Openness

Tim Berners-Lee made a crucial decision: he did not patent Web technologies or establish a closed standards body. Instead, he made the specifications for HTTP, HTML, and URLs fully open. Anyone could use, modify, or extend them for free.

That was unusual at the time. In the 1990s, many companies were trying to build their own “information superhighways,” but most were closed, paid systems that required a dedicated client.

The Web's openness allowed it to stand out in the competition. **Openness meant no single point of control, no rent-seeking, and no barrier to entry.** Anyone could build a website; anyone could visit one; there was no intermediary taking a cut.

### 3. Permissionlessness

This is one of the Web's most powerful features: **you do not need anyone's approval to create a website, publish your content, or link to another website.**

There was no review, no gatekeeping, and no “pay first, then go live.” All you needed was a server and a domain name to join the global network.

This permissionless quality released enormous creative energy. From personal blogs to Wikipedia, from GitHub to YouTube, countless great innovations began as websites that did not require anyone's approval.

**Permissionlessness meant that the speed of innovation was no longer limited by approval processes, but only by the creator's imagination.**

### 4. Built on Existing Infrastructure

HTTP did not try to reinvent the wheel. It was built on TCP/IP, used DNS for domain-name resolution, and relied on the Internet infrastructure that already existed.

This meant the Web could work globally from day one. You did not have to wait for a new network protocol to be deployed, new hardware to be manufactured, or a new standard to be approved. If you could get online, you could access the Web.

**Building on existing infrastructure dramatically reduced deployment costs and accelerated adoption.**

### The Essential Achievement: Liberating the Production and Distribution of Information

The core achievement of Web 1.0 was to **liberate the production and distribution of information**.

Before that, publishing information required publishers, printing presses, and distribution channels. The Web allowed anyone to become a publisher.

But this era had one defining feature: **people were still the primary “interpreters” and “executors.”**

Web pages were for people to read, hyperlinks were for people to click, and information was for people to process. The entire Web was designed around human cognitive and operational capabilities.

**People remained the primary productive force.**

That fact planted the seed for the platform transformation that followed.

## III. Web 2.0: Platformization Was Not a Betrayal, but a Choice for Efficiency

If the keywords of Web 1.0 were “openness” and “connection,” then the keywords of Web 2.0 were “platforms” and “user-generated content.”

In the early 2000s, as broadband spread and technology advanced, the Internet moved from “read-only” to “read-write.” Blogs, forums, and social networks took off. Users were no longer passive consumers of information; they became active creators.

This was supposed to be a good thing. But it was accompanied by a profound transfer of power.

### Why Did Platforms Appear?

Many people blame the rise of platforms on commercial greed or moral decline, but that explanation is too simple.

Platforms emerged in response to three real problems:

#### 1. Information Overload

Web 1.0 successfully solved the problem of publishing information, but it created a new one: there was too much information.

When the Internet contained billions of web pages, people could not find what they needed on their own. Search engines emerged to solve this problem.

Google's PageRank algorithm essentially used “connection” to measure the value of information: a page linked by more high-quality pages was often more valuable.

**This was the first distortion of “connection is power”: connection was no longer equal; it had weight.**

#### 2. The Cost of Managing Relationships Rose Sharply

In the Web 1.0 era, you might follow dozens of blogs through RSS. But when your contacts reached hundreds or thousands, managing relationships manually became impossible.

Social networks emerged to solve this problem. Facebook, Twitter, and WeChat help manage relationships: who your friends are, whose content you might find interesting, and who is online.

**Platforms became organizers and intermediaries of relationships.**

#### 3. The Complexity of Matching Transactions

The rise of e-commerce created new needs: How could buyers find sellers? How could transactions be made safe? How could disputes be resolved?

Taobao, Amazon, and eBay emerged to solve these problems. They provided an entire suite of services: search, payments, logistics, ratings, and after-sales support.

**Platforms became matchmakers for transactions and intermediaries of trust.**

### The Internal Logic of Platformization

Ultimately, platforms solved three core problems:

1. **Content filtering** (search and recommendation)
2. **Relationship organization** (social graphs)
3. **Transaction matching** (e-commerce and advertising)

These three problems had one common characteristic: **they were all optimized for human attention and operational capacity.**

Human attention is limited, and so is our ability to operate. When the scale of information and relationships exceeds our capacity to process them, some kind of intermediary is needed to filter, organize, and match.

**The platform is that intermediary.**

This is not a moral question, but an efficiency question. In an era when humans were the primary productive force, platformization was the inevitable choice.

## IV. The Internal Drivers of Enclosure and Monopoly

The emergence of platforms was not itself the problem. The question is why platforms went on to become closed and monopolistic.

The answer lies in the economics of software.

### The Marginal Cost of Software Approaches Zero

In traditional manufacturing, producing a product requires raw materials, labor, and equipment. The cost of producing 100 units is roughly 100 times the cost of producing one.

Software is different. Developing software costs money, but copying it costs almost nothing. The cost difference between serving 100 users and serving 100 million users is far less than a factor of one million.

**This means software naturally has economies of scale.**

### The Larger the Scale, the Greater the Value

For platform software, the effect of scale is even more pronounced:

- **More users** → produce more data
- **More data** → train better algorithms
- **Better algorithms** → deliver a better experience
- **A better experience** → attract more users

This creates a positive-feedback flywheel:

> **More users → higher efficiency → stronger lock-in → a larger platform**

This is why the Internet industry so easily produces winner-take-all outcomes.

### Enclosure Is the Inevitable Result of Economies of Scale

Once a platform becomes dominant, it has strong reasons to close itself off:

1. **Data is a core asset**: opening data means giving competitors free access to your core assets.
2. **User lock-in is a moat**: making it difficult for users to leave is key to maintaining a monopoly.
3. **Control of the ecosystem is a source of power**: controlling the developer ecosystem means controlling the direction of innovation.

WeChat can decide which links may be shared and which may not; Taobao can decide which products are recommended and which are not; the App Store can decide which applications may be listed.

### The High Cost of Openness

Choosing openness, by contrast, creates enormous cost pressures.

There are open businesses in the information space. Email, voice calls, and text messages are typical open-Internet applications. But they share one characteristic: **they are simple.**

Email protocols (SMTP/POP3/IMAP) were born in the 1970s and 1980s. Their core function is simply to send and receive text messages. Voice calls and text messages are similar: their functions are limited and their standards are clear.

But as a business becomes more complex, the cost of openness rises sharply:

- Implementations from different vendors must remain compatible.
- Protocol upgrades require coordination among all participants.
- Accidental complexity compounds with essential complexity.
- Debugging and maintenance costs grow exponentially.

Imagine if WeChat's Moments, Channels, payments, and mini-programs all had to interoperate openly with other platforms. How many standards would be needed? How many rounds of negotiation? How many rounds of testing? It would be a **massive software-engineering disaster**.

**Therefore, as Internet businesses became more complex, openness declined. This was not a moral choice, but an engineering reality.**

### A Key Judgment

In the traditional software era, there was a basic logic:

> **Develop software once, serve everyone; the more users there are, the greater the efficiency; monopoly is not accidental, but an endogenous result.**

This is not to say platform monopoly is justified. It is to say that **if the underlying technology does not change, the Internet cannot automatically return to openness.**

The histories of the Semantic Web and Web3 prove this point.

## V. Why Didn't the Semantic Web Break Enclosure?

As Web 2.0 moved toward enclosure, another path was being explored: the Semantic Web.

In 1998, Tim Berners-Lee proposed a vision for the Semantic Web: enabling machines to understand the meaning of web pages rather than merely display them. By adding semantic annotations to data, machines could automatically reason over, integrate, and analyze information, creating a truly intelligent network.

Does that sound familiar? Isn't that what AI agents are trying to do today?

I recommend reading the article on the Semantic Web that Tim Berners-Lee published in *Scientific American*. It contains a concrete description of an agent network that is remarkably similar to today's vision of an agent network.

But the Semantic Web never achieved mass adoption. Why?

At root, the reasons can be summarized in three points: **the cost of manual annotation was too high, commercial incentives were lacking, and the technology stack was too complex.**

The Semantic Web required website developers to annotate data with languages such as RDF and OWL. But this brought operators no direct benefit: users could not see the annotations, and search engines did not offer higher rankings for them. More importantly, when the technical barrier was too high and commercial companies were not driving adoption, an ecosystem could not form and network effects could not take off.

The Semantic Web left us with an important lesson:

> **Helping machines understand the semantics of data is the right direction, but it cannot depend on manual annotation; it should depend on machines understanding data automatically.**

That is precisely the revolutionary change brought by large language models.

But before AI appeared, the Semantic Web was destined to fail—because it tried to use an “institutional or technological ideal” to fight the productive reality of its time.

---

## VI. Why Didn't Web3 Break Enclosure?

Web3 tried to solve platform monopoly with blockchain: putting user data on-chain, running applications through smart contracts, and establishing ownership of digital assets through NFTs. The vision was beautiful, but it also failed to deliver.

The problems were threefold: **it reduced trust to an on-chain problem** (real-world trust also involves execution, accountability, and disputes); **its performance bottlenecks were difficult to overcome** (Bitcoin processes seven transactions per second, versus Alipay's 100,000); and **the user experience was too poor** (seed phrases, private keys, and gas fees put off ordinary users).

The deeper problem of Web3 was the same as that of the Semantic Web: **it tried to use an “institutional or technological ideal” to fight the productive reality of its time.** In an era when humans were the primary productive force, managing private keys, paying gas fees, and waiting for block confirmations were unacceptable costs. Web3 did not solve the problem of “who will act”—humans were still the primary executors, but human ability and willingness were not enough to support the decentralized vision.

---

## Conclusion: The Evolution of the Web Was Not a Mistake—Each Era Found Its Optimal Solution

Returning to the question at the beginning: **Why did a network born to enable “connection” inevitably become platformized and closed?**

The answer is: **because in every era, people chose the optimal solution under the productive conditions of that time.**

- Web 1.0 liberated the production of information, but created information overload.
- Web 2.0 organized massive amounts of information, but produced platform monopolies.
- The Semantic Web tried to make machines understand data, but depended on manual annotation, making the cost too high.
- Web3 tried to decentralize the Internet with blockchain, but ignored user experience and efficiency.

**Enclosure was not a conspiracy. In an era when humans were the primary productive force, it was the inevitable result of efficiency and economies of scale.**

This gives us an important lesson:

> **If the productive forces do not undergo a fundamental change, the Internet cannot automatically return to openness.**

So what kind of change in productive forces could break this cycle?

The answer is: **when the executor changes from a human to an AI agent.**

AI agents do not need graphical user interfaces, are not constrained by attention, do not care about the complexity of managing private keys, and are not afraid of information overload. They can communicate directly through protocols, discover and connect to services autonomously, and negotiate and execute tasks automatically.

**This is the historical opportunity of the Agentic Web.**
